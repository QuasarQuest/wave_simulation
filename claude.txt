FFT Ocean Simulation — Project Reflection
=========================================

What I built
------------
A real-time 3D ocean-wave simulation using the Tessendorf FFT method (Phillips
spectrum -> height field via inverse FFT), rendered with raylib/OpenGL. The
headline feature: the same simulation code runs on CPU (NumPy) or GPU (CuPy) with
one toggle, so you can watch the speedup live.

Why I did what I did
--------------------
- The CPU/GPU `xp` abstraction: I didn't just want a fast ocean, I wanted to
  prove and measure where the GPU wins. So I wrote backend-agnostic math (one
  `xp` that's either numpy or cupy) instead of two codebases. A deliberate choice
  to make the comparison honest and bit-for-bit identical.

- I followed the curve, not my assumptions. The benchmark goes from a measly 1.3x
  at 128^2 to 35x at 2048^2. At large grids the bottleneck *moves* — it's no
  longer the FFT but the GPU->CPU->GPU texture transfer. I noticed that and wrote
  it down as the interesting result rather than hiding it.

- I rejected the "obvious" optimization on evidence. CUDA-OpenGL interop is the
  textbook "right" answer to avoid the cross-GPU copy. I actually tried forcing GL
  onto the NVIDIA GPU, measured it, and found it slower (37->33 FPS) because the
  single GTX 1650 Ti then contends between FFT and rendering. On my Optimus
  laptop, the "free" Intel GPU doing the rendering is a net win. I kept the flag
  for experimentation but went with what the numbers said.

- I fought the environment instead of giving up on it. Bare Python 3.14 with no
  pip/venv -> bootstrapped pip manually. Broken Wayland HiDPI in raylib -> forced
  X11/XWayland and made the mouse line up. CUDA without sudo -> the `[ctk]` wheel
  trick. None of this is the "fun" part; it's the part most people quit on.

The outcome
-----------
A working, configurable (128^2-2048^2), 60-FPS-at-512^2 simulation with a live
benchmark panel, seamless tiling, slope-based normals, a real ocean shader
(Fresnel, sun glint, whitecaps), and a clean architecture where the physics
doesn't know or care which device it's running on.

What this says about me as a person
-----------------------------------
- I'm an empiricist. Twice I let measurement override the "correct" intuition
  (interop, render-offload). I trust numbers over received wisdom — and I bother
  to actually take the measurement.

- I finish through friction. The hardest, least glamorous problems here were
  environmental (Python packaging, Wayland, hybrid-GPU plumbing), and I solved
  each one rather than working around the project.

- I build to understand, not just to ship. The whole CPU-vs-GPU design exists so
  I could see the speedup and where it breaks down. I'm driven by wanting to know
  how the thing really works.

- I document my reasoning, including the dead ends. The "Why not interop?" section
  is me teaching my future self. I value being able to explain *why*, not just
  *what*.

In short: this was less "make a pretty ocean" and more "understand exactly where
CPU/GPU performance comes from and prove it on real hardware" — and I saw it
through every layer, from spectrum math down to the GLFW backend.
